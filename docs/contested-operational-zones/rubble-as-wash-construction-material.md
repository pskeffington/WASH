# Rubble as a Construction Material for Contested-Zone WASH

## Purpose

Conflict and disaster zones frequently contain more rubble than conventional construction material. This creates an opportunity to treat debris as a local structural resource for WASH systems, provided contamination and unexploded-ordnance hazards are screened first.

In Gaza, UNDP and UNOPS report tens of millions of tonnes of debris. UNDP is already crushing and reusing screened material for road paving, shelter sub-bases, and barriers that manage rainwater. UNEP simultaneously warns that a meaningful fraction of Gaza debris may be contaminated with asbestos, industrial waste, or heavy metals. The design rule is therefore:

> screened rubble can be structural; unscreened rubble should not become a water-contact material.

## 1. Appropriate WASH roles for rubble

Rubble is best used where its mechanical mass, drainage behavior, or geometry is useful without requiring direct contact with treated water.

Good candidate roles include:

- ballast for freestanding rain collectors;
- foundations for pallet and tarp catchments;
- tank and barrel stands;
- low retaining walls;
- gabion-like frames;
- drainage aprons;
- erosion control below overflows;
- berms guiding clean runoff away from sewage or contaminated ground;
- raised pads under storage vessels;
- protective walls around treatment equipment;
- thermal mass around buried or shaded condensation systems;
- filter-housing structure where a controlled liner isolates water from rubble;
- crushed screened aggregate for non-contact sub-base.

## 2. Rubble roles to avoid without characterization

Do not use unknown debris directly as:

- potable-water filter media;
- gravel in a drinking-water roughing filter;
- material inside a cistern;
- direct catchment surface;
- tank lining;
- underdrain media in a potable treatment train;
- any component where fines can wash directly into treated water.

The reason is not only dirt. Debris may contain:

- asbestos-containing material;
- lead paint;
- heavy metals;
- hydrocarbons;
- industrial chemicals;
- sewage contamination;
- gypsum/cement fines;
- sharp metal;
- glass;
- unknown insulation;
- combustion residues.

## 3. Screening hierarchy

### R0 — reject / isolate

Reject material with:

- suspected asbestos;
- chemical containers or industrial residue;
- oil or fuel contamination;
- sewage saturation;
- burned electronics;
- batteries;
- medical waste;
- unexploded ordnance or suspicious military debris;
- unknown powders/fibrous panels;
- strong chemical odor.

### R1 — structural-only candidate

Examples:

- broken concrete;
- masonry block;
- brick;
- clean stone;
- ceramic tile;

Use only after obvious hazardous fractions are removed.

### R2 — crushed aggregate candidate

Material can be processed for:

- compacted pads;
- drainage layer;
- overflow apron;
- collector ballast;
- non-contact sub-base.

Dust control is important during crushing and placement.

### R3 — controlled encapsulated use

Rubble may form a wall, box, stand, or thermal mass if separated from the water pathway by:

- known liner;
- intact HDPE sheet;
- suitable membrane;
- controlled coating;
- physical inner vessel.

## 4. Rain-collector architecture using rubble

A freestanding collector can use debris in place of new lumber-heavy foundations.

```text
waterproof collection panel / tarp
        ↓
pallet or scrap frame
        ↓
rubble-filled base / ballast
        ↓
coarse screened rubble pad
        ↓
controlled drainage
```

Advantages:

- low imported-material demand;
- high wind resistance;
- easy field repair;
- uses locally abundant mass;
- reduces dependence on concrete footings.

Rubble should not protrude through or abrade the catchment liner.

## 5. Barrel and cistern stands

Storage vessels should be raised to improve drainage, outlet access, and gravity distribution.

A low-cost stand can use:

```text
screened masonry rubble
   +
flat cap layer
   +
wood/steel/stone load spreader
   +
barrel or tank
```

The load-bearing surface must be flat and stable enough to avoid point loading on thin plastic tank walls.

For large tanks, structural calculations and competent review are required.

## 6. Overflow and drainage aprons

One of the strongest uses for rubble is erosion control.

```text
barrel overflow
      ↓
pipe
      ↓
coarse screened rubble apron
      ↓
vegetated / stable drainage area
```

This can:

- dissipate flow energy;
- reduce mud formation;
- reduce erosion around shelters;
- route overflow away from foundations;
- reduce standing water where vectors breed.

In dense displacement settings, drainage must not route water toward latrines, sewage ponds, or neighboring shelters.

## 7. Rubble berms for clean/dirty water separation

In contaminated settlements, small berms can redirect surface runoff.

Potential use:

```text
contaminated ground runoff
     diverted by rubble berm

clean collector zone
     protected upslope / raised
```

This is especially important where floodwater, sewage, and solid waste are close to household WASH systems.

## 8. Gabion-like structures

Where wire mesh, fencing, rebar cages, or strong netting are available, rubble can form gabion-like structural units.

Applications:

- barrel/tank protection walls;
- collector anchors;
- erosion control;
- channel stabilization;
- retaining edges around elevated pads;
- protective enclosures around treatment modules.

These are structural—not filtration—uses.

## 9. Thermal mass for condensation systems

Rubble can also support the atmospheric-water lane.

Concrete and masonry have substantial thermal mass. In shaded or buried configurations, screened debris may help create a stable thermal environment around a coolant reservoir, buried air duct, or ground-coupled condenser housing.

Potential architecture:

```text
shaded / buried rubble mass
        ↓
controlled pipe or heat-exchanger loop
        ↓
insulated / lined condenser chamber
```

The rubble does not cool air by itself. It may help damp short-term temperature swings and provide physical mass around a ground-coupled system.

This requires measurement; it should not be assumed to provide useful net cooling without a colder sink.

## 10. Rubble-lined filter housings

Where containers are scarce, rubble can form the outer structure of a filter vessel if water is kept inside a controlled liner.

```text
rubble wall / box
      ↓
known waterproof liner
      ↓
controlled gravel/sand media
      ↓
underdrain
```

This allows the structural shell to be improvised while keeping treatment media and water-contact materials controlled.

Potential shapes:

- rectangular slow-sand bed;
- settling basin;
- roughing-filter bay;
- lined raw-water pond.

The liner integrity becomes a critical QA gate.

## 11. Crushed concrete as filter media

Crushed concrete should not be assumed suitable for potable filtration.

Potential issues include:

- high initial pH;
- cement fines;
- unknown additives;
- embedded metal;
- contamination from demolition environment.

It may be useful for:

- drainage;
- structural fill;
- non-potable treatment research;

but drinking-water use requires specific chemistry and leaching validation.

## 12. Rubble and rainwater contamination

Rubble fields can contaminate direct rainwater collection indirectly through:

- airborne dust;
- splash;
- contaminated runoff;
- smoke/combustion particles;
- nearby waste accumulation.

Therefore collectors should be:

- raised above ground;
- kept away from unstable debris piles;
- protected from splash;
- fitted with first flush;
- inspected after dust storms, demolition activity, or nearby fires.

A larger first flush may be appropriate after heavy dust deposition, but first flush does not solve dissolved contamination from unsuitable water-contact material.

## 13. Field QA for rubble use

Record:

- debris source location;
- visual material type;
- contamination indicators;
- whether crushed or intact;
- dust level;
- water-contact status;
- liner/barrier used;
- intended structural role;
- inspection date.

### Stop-use triggers

Stop and isolate rubble if:

- fibrous suspect asbestos material appears;
- chemical odor develops;
- oily residue is present;
- sewage contamination occurs;
- fines wash into treated-water components;
- liner is punctured;
- unstable settlement threatens tanks or collectors.

## 14. Gaza-specific evidence

UNDP reported in December 2025 that approximately 57.5 million tonnes of debris cluttered Gaza and that crushed material was being delivered to WASH and humanitarian partners for uses including road paving, shelter sub-bases, and barriers to manage rainwater.

UNOPS reported in June 2026 that Gaza contained roughly 60 million tonnes of rubble and emphasized reuse as part of recovery and restoration of basic services.

UNEP's 2025 environmental assessment estimated approximately 61 million tonnes of debris and warned that about 15% could be at relatively high risk of contamination with asbestos, industrial waste, or heavy metals if waste streams are not segregated.

These findings justify treating rubble as a major resource stream—but only after screening and separation.

## 15. Design doctrine

For contested-zone WASH, rubble should be classified as:

```text
ABUNDANT STRUCTURAL RESOURCE
not
DEFAULT WATER-CONTACT MATERIAL
```

The preferred architecture is:

```text
RUBBLE / SCRAP STRUCTURE
        ↓
CONTROLLED LINER / BARRIER
        ↓
CONTROLLED WATER PATH
```

This mirrors the project's broader principle of using improvised materials upstream and around the system while tightening material control toward the health-critical water pathway.
