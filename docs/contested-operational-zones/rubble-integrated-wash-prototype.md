# Rubble-Integrated WASH Prototype Architecture

## Purpose

This prototype turns screened conflict debris into a structural resource for decentralized WASH systems while keeping potable and treatment-critical water paths isolated from unknown rubble chemistry.

The concept is intentionally modular:

```text
screened rubble = structure / ballast / drainage / protection
controlled liner + known vessels = water-contact path
```

The prototype is designed around a four-pallet rain collector and a small barrel bank, with optional expansion into lined filtration bays and a separate condensation research module.

---

## 1. Evidence basis

Gaza contains approximately 60 million tonnes of rubble according to 2026 UNOPS reporting. UNDP is already crushing and reusing screened debris for road paving, shelter sub-bases, and barriers that manage rainwater. UNEP has simultaneously warned that roughly 15% of Gaza debris could be at relatively high risk of contamination with asbestos, industrial waste, or heavy metals if waste streams are not segregated.

Therefore this prototype does **not** treat rubble as inherently safe. It assumes rubble has passed a basic debris-screening gate and is used outside the controlled water-contact pathway.

---

## 2. Prototype family

### R-WASH-P1 — four-pallet collector + rubble ballast

Core elements:

- four 4x4-ft pallet or scrap-frame catchment panels;
- known waterproof liner or suitable barrier surface;
- screened gutter and first-flush module;
- screened rubble foundation/ballast;
- 2-4 known-use barrels;
- rubble overflow apron.

Conceptual arrangement:

```text
      PANEL A      PANEL B
          \        /
           \      /
            GUTTER
              ↓
          FIRST FLUSH
              ↓
        STORAGE BANK

      PANEL C      PANEL D
          \        /
           \      /
            HEADER
```

All panel frames are independently anchored so damage to one does not collapse the full array.

## 3. Rubble foundation

### Function

The rubble base provides:

- ballast against wind;
- a level working platform;
- drainage above muddy ground;
- separation between collector feet and contaminated surface runoff;
- a reusable sub-base where timber or concrete is scarce.

### Recommended architecture

```text
native ground
    ↓
coarse screened rubble
    ↓
smaller screened aggregate / flat masonry
    ↓
load-spreading timber / stone / steel plate
    ↓
collector frame
```

Avoid direct point loading from sharp rubble onto plastic or thin wooden members.

### Geometry

Initial prototype target:

- pad footprint: roughly 3 x 3 m for a compact four-panel collector;
- pad depth: 150-300 mm depending on ground condition;
- larger angular rubble on bottom;
- flatter fragments near the top;
- slight outward drainage slope.

These dimensions are prototype starting points, not certified structural values.

---

## 4. Collector anchoring

A freestanding collector in an exposed zone needs substantial ballast.

Possible anchor methods:

### A. rubble-filled crate

```text
wood / wire crate
    + screened masonry rubble
    + tied collector leg
```

### B. gabion-like anchor

```text
wire mesh / fencing
    ↓
rubble fill
    ↓
frame tie point
```

### C. rubble pocket

A shallow excavated pocket can hold coarse rubble around a protected frame foot.

The frame should remain removable and repairable. Do not cast the collector permanently into unknown rubble unless long-term placement is intended.

---

## 5. Barrel-bank stand

### Goal

Raise storage enough for:

- gravity draw-off;
- inspection;
- bottom drainage access;
- separation from floodwater and contaminated soil.

### Architecture

```text
screened rubble pad
      ↓
flat cap layer
      ↓
load-spreading slab / plank / plate
      ↓
barrel bank
```

### Initial scale

For 55-gallon / ~208-L barrels:

- stand height: approximately 200-400 mm;
- one barrel per isolated support bay where possible;
- secondary restraint against tipping;
- no direct sharp-rubble contact with the barrel bottom.

A filled 208-L barrel weighs over 200 kg, so larger communal tanks require structural review rather than simple extrapolation.

---

## 6. Contamination-isolated barrel bank

Preferred arrangement:

```text
BARREL A   BARREL B   BARREL C   BARREL D
   |           |          |          |
individual valves / removable connections
```

Avoid permanent common-bottom plumbing where practical.

Advantages:

- isolate a contaminated barrel;
- move one vessel independently;
- assign different sources to different vessels;
- clean one barrel without draining the whole bank.

---

## 7. Rubble overflow apron

Overflow should never create a stagnant contaminated pool beneath the storage area.

### Architecture

```text
barrel overflow pipe
       ↓
energy-dissipation stone
       ↓
coarse screened rubble apron
       ↓
stable drainage path
```

### Prototype geometry

Initial test dimensions:

- apron length: 1-2 m;
- width: 0.5-1 m;
- coarse rubble layer: ~150-250 mm;
- discharge away from shelters, latrines, sewage, and foundations.

The apron should be treated as drainage infrastructure, not filtration.

---

## 8. Rubble berm for clean-zone protection

A low berm can protect the collector/storage pad from contaminated ground runoff.

```text
upslope dirty runoff
      ↓
   RUBBLE BERM
      ↓ redirected

raised WASH pad
```

This is especially useful where rain events mobilize sewage, waste, or fine debris.

The collector should receive direct rainfall, not runoff from the rubble field.

---

## 9. Rubble-based lined filter bay

Where barrels are scarce, screened rubble can form the external shell of a settling or slow-filter bed.

### Concept

```text
rubble retaining wall
       ↓
protective fabric / smooth layer
       ↓
known waterproof liner
       ↓
controlled filter media
       ↓
known underdrain
```

Rubble must remain outside the liner.

Possible modules:

- settling basin;
- horizontal roughing-filter bay;
- slow-sand filter;
- emergency raw-water reservoir.

### Gate

Liner integrity must be inspectable. Any puncture is a stop-use condition for potable research.

---

## 10. Rubble thermal-mass condensation module

Gaza summer humidity and dew-point conditions justify investigating a separate condensation lane.

Rubble may help by providing:

- shaded thermal mass;
- burial mass around an air duct;
- structural protection for coolant piping;
- temperature buffering around a condenser enclosure.

### Concept

```text
shade / rubble enclosure
       ↓
buried or protected air pre-cooler
       ↓
controlled heat exchanger
       ↓
condensate tray
       ↓
separate collection vessel
```

The rubble is not assumed to create cooling by itself. Useful condensation still requires a surface below dew point and a sustainable heat sink.

### First measurement gate

Measure:

- ambient temperature/RH;
- rubble-core temperature at multiple depths;
- shaded soil temperature;
- intake and outlet air temperature;
- condenser surface temperature;
- condensate volume.

If the rubble mass does not remain colder than ambient during productive periods, it should not be credited as a cooling source.

## 11. Rubble screening gate

Before WASH use, debris should be assigned one of four categories.

### RR-0 — reject

- suspected asbestos;
- industrial residue;
- medical waste;
- electronics/batteries;
- fuel/oil contamination;
- sewage-saturated material;
- unknown fibrous panels;
- explosive or suspicious ordnance-related debris.

### RR-1 — structural only

- visually clean broken concrete;
- masonry block;
- brick;
- stone;
- ceramic tile.

### RR-2 — crush/reuse candidate

Material suitable for controlled processing into:

- pad aggregate;
- drainage apron;
- road/sub-base;
- berm fill.

### RR-3 — encapsulated construction

Rubble used behind a known liner or barrier for:

- filter shell;
- tank enclosure;
- thermal-mass structure.

---

## 12. Dust control

Rubble handling can create substantial particulate exposure.

Avoid dry crushing or aggressive disturbance near:

- stored water;
- open filters;
- food preparation;
- shelters;
- medical areas.

Collector surfaces should be cleaned and first-flush volume reassessed after major debris movement or dust events.

---

## 13. Deployment sequence

### Gate R1 — site screen

- identify sewage/flood pathways;
- inspect rubble for hazardous fractions;
- identify stable ground;
- map overflow route.

### Gate R2 — pad build

- place screened rubble;
- establish level load-bearing surface;
- create outward drainage.

### Gate R3 — collector erection

- assemble panels;
- install controlled liner;
- anchor using rubble mass;
- verify slope and drainage.

### Gate R4 — storage installation

- build raised rubble stand;
- install load spreaders;
- place known-use barrels;
- verify isolation valves.

### Gate R5 — hydraulic test

- simulate rainfall or use first storm;
- inspect leaks;
- confirm first flush;
- confirm barrel fill sequence;
- confirm safe overflow.

### Gate R6 — contamination control

- inspect dust and splash pathways;
- verify no rubble contacts stored water;
- sample raw/treated water as required.

### Gate R7 — maintenance

- inspect ballast settlement;
- check liner abrasion;
- clear drainage apron;
- inspect barrel stability.

---

## 14. Material-import minimization

The rubble-integrated architecture deliberately reserves scarce imported or controlled materials for the water-critical pathway.

### Local / salvage mass

- screened rubble;
- pallet/scrap wood;
- wire/fencing;
- stone;
- masonry.

### Controlled imported / known materials

- water-contact liner;
- PVC/fittings;
- screen;
- first-flush hardware;
- treatment element;
- disinfection supplies;
- test supplies.

This can materially reduce logistics burden while preserving water-contact control.

---

## 15. Evidence anchors

UNOPS reported in June 2026 that Gaza contained more than 60 million tonnes of rubble and emphasized debris reuse as a pathway to restoring basic services including water infrastructure. UNDP's December 2025 debris report documented crushed rubble being delivered to WASH and humanitarian partners for road paving, shelter sub-bases, and barriers to manage rainwater. UNEP reported in September 2025 that about 15% of the estimated 61 million tonnes of debris could be at relatively high contamination risk if waste streams are not segregated, including possible asbestos, industrial waste, and heavy metals.

These sources support a screened-reuse approach rather than blanket reuse.
