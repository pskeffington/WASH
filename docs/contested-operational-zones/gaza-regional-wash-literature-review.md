# Gaza-Centered Literature Review: WASH in Contested Operational Zones

## Scope

This review establishes the Gaza Strip as the primary case study for the WASH in Contested Operational Zones sector. Gaza is useful analytically because it combines nearly every major stressor relevant to contested-zone WASH:

- damaged water and wastewater infrastructure;
- severe electricity and fuel dependence;
- degraded groundwater quality;
- desalination dependence;
- damaged distribution networks;
- mass displacement;
- constrained humanitarian access;
- limited spare parts and consumables;
- sewage and solid-waste accumulation;
- winter flooding;
- public-health surveillance burden;
- dependence on trucking and temporary storage;
- high population density.

The review uses Gaza as the prime case while drawing comparisons from Lebanon, Syria, and Yemen to identify transferable WASH resilience principles.

The sector remains actor-neutral and civilian-outcome focused. The objective is to support legitimate responders preserving civilian water, sanitation, and hygiene access under severe infrastructure disruption.

---

# 1. Gaza as a coupled WASH-system failure case

A central lesson from Gaza is that water supply cannot be separated from energy, wastewater, distribution, transport, sanitation, and public health.

In 2026, UNICEF and ICRC reporting described severe damage across water infrastructure, desalination, sewage systems, pipelines, wells, and pumping stations. UNICEF reported that nearly 90% of water and sanitation infrastructure had been damaged or destroyed by mid-2026. ICRC reporting in June 2026 cited local water-utility estimates that more than 80% of infrastructure needed to clean, store, and distribute water was damaged, destroyed, or inaccessible.

At the same time, water production had fallen sharply relative to pre-conflict conditions. ICRC reported production around 40% of pre-October-2023 levels in June 2026, while independent production from Gaza wells was less than one-third of previous levels.

This demonstrates an important systems principle:

```text
WATER AVAILABILITY
is not only a source problem

it is also:
energy
+ pumping
+ treatment
+ distribution
+ storage
+ transport
+ wastewater control
+ access
```

The contested-zone WASH architecture must therefore be designed around **system redundancy**, not simply around finding another source.

---

# 2. Minimum water availability and survival thresholds

UNICEF reported in July 2026 that an estimated 82% of Gaza families were water insecure and that up to 70% were unable to obtain even 6 litres per person per day for drinking and cooking.

ICRC reporting in June 2026 described approximately 6 litres per person per day reaching many people, below commonly cited emergency WASH planning values and below the roughly 7.5 litres per person per day baseline often associated with minimum survival drinking/cooking needs.

The major design implication is that very small decentralized systems can still matter in a severe shortage.

For example:

```text
8 gallons from one 4x4-ft pallet collector
≈ 30 litres
```

That quantity is not sufficient for complete household WASH needs, but during acute scarcity it can represent meaningful supplemental water for a family if appropriately treated and used.

This supports measuring contested-zone technologies by:

```text
litres produced
per person
per rainfall event
per kg of imported material
per dollar
per labour-hour
```

rather than only by total system capacity.

# 3. Groundwater limitation: salinity and nitrate

The Gaza Coastal Aquifer has long been under severe stress independent of the current conflict.

A 2023 peer-reviewed study in *Heliyon* found persistent chemical contamination and reported that groundwater salinity had increased approximately 30% over two decades, largely associated with seawater intrusion.

Earlier hydrogeochemical work found widespread nitrate pollution and strong salinity effects associated with seawater intrusion, saltwater up-coning, wastewater inputs, and over-pumping.

A 2019 Gaza desalination case study described groundwater chloride concentrations around 600-2,000 mg/L and nitrate around 50-200 mg/L in many areas, exceeding drinking-water guidance values.

The consequence is critical:

```text
A WELL IS NOT AUTOMATICALLY A SAFE FALLBACK
```

In Gaza-like coastal conflict environments, emergency well development may produce water that requires desalination and/or nitrate treatment rather than only microbial disinfection.

For the WASH repo this means source characterization must distinguish:

- microbial contamination;
- salinity;
- nitrate;
- sewage intrusion;
- industrial or fire-related contaminants;
- material-associated metals.

A biosand filter or chlorination step cannot solve seawater salinity.

# 4. Desalination dependence and fragility

Gaza has depended on desalination for drinking-water production for decades because of poor groundwater quality.

Peer-reviewed literature from the early 2000s through 2019 repeatedly identifies desalination as a strategic or necessary component of Gaza's water supply.

By 2026 desalination remained indispensable, but the conflict exposed major operational dependencies.

UNICEF reported in May 2026 that seawater desalination output fell from approximately 20,000 m3/day in March to 16,000 m3/day because of shortages of chemicals and spare parts.

In March 2026, damage to an electricity line serving the Southern Gaza Seawater Desalination Plant reduced output by approximately 80% until repairs restored service.

The operational dependency chain is therefore:

```text
SOURCE WATER
   ↓
DESALINATION PLANT
   ↓
ELECTRICITY
   ↓
PUMPS
   ↓
MEMBRANES / CHEMICALS
   ↓
SPARE PARTS / LUBRICANTS
   ↓
DISTRIBUTION
```

Failure at any node can collapse output.

## Design implication

Large desalination remains necessary for regional-scale supply, but contested-zone resilience should add smaller decentralized sources and storage so the population is not exposed to one failure mode.

This does not mean improvised desalination is generally desirable. Reverse osmosis requires controlled membranes, pressure, pretreatment, maintenance, and brine management.

A more defensible architecture is:

```text
CENTRAL DESALINATION
        +
DECENTRALIZED COLLECTION
        +
HOUSEHOLD / COMMUNITY STORAGE
        +
LOW-POWER TREATMENT
```

# 5. Energy-water coupling

Gaza illustrates the water-energy nexus unusually clearly.

ICRC reporting has repeatedly documented the dependence of wells, boreholes, desalination plants, sewage pumping stations, and treatment systems on electricity and generator fuel.

In 2026 Gaza had experienced prolonged absence of regular grid electricity, leaving many WASH systems dependent on generators, fuel, oil, and spare parts.

Comparable patterns appear elsewhere in the region:

- In Syria, UNICEF reported in January 2026 that electricity damage left roughly one million people in Al-Hassakeh affected by power and water outages, while approximately 500,000 people around Ain Al Arab/Kobane were relying entirely on generators for electricity and water.
- In Lebanon, UNICEF has invested heavily in solar-powered water systems because economic crisis and conflict have undermined reliable pumping and utility operations.

## Design implication

Contested-zone WASH systems should be ranked by their energy burden:

### Tier E0 — no external energy

- rainwater catchment;
- gravity settling;
- gravity roughing filters;
- biosand;
- ceramic filtration;
- gravity distribution;
- some solar disinfection approaches.

### Tier E1 — very low electrical demand

- small DC pumps;
- low-power UV;
- small membrane systems;
- sensor/monitoring equipment.

### Tier E2 — significant energy demand

- pressurized membrane filtration;
- reverse osmosis;
- borehole pumping;
- large-scale desalination.

This makes no-power systems strategically useful as resilience layers even when they cannot supply all demand.

# 6. Water trucking as both lifeline and vulnerability

Water trucking is repeatedly documented across Gaza and other conflict-affected regions.

UNICEF's May 2026 Gaza update described drinking-water distribution reaching up to 1.5 million people depending on access and operational conditions.

The same report documented suspension of trucking from the Al Mansoura filling point after two contracted truck drivers were killed in April 2026. UNICEF then had to truck water from desalination plants at an additional cost of approximately USD 40,000 per day to replace about 2 million litres per day previously collected from the filling point.

This creates another resilience lesson:

```text
TRUCKING IS ESSENTIAL
but
TRUCKING IS A LOGISTICS-DEPENDENT SOURCE
```

Truck-dependent systems require:

- access routes;
- fuel;
- drivers;
- filling points;
- vehicles;
- storage at destination;
- security/access conditions.

## Design implication

Decentralized household and community collection can reduce—but not eliminate—dependence on trucking.

The relevant research question is not:

```text
Can micro-catchment replace trucking?
```

but:

```text
How many trucked litres can local collection offset during productive periods?
```

# 7. Storage as a critical resilience asset

In infrastructure failure, storage often becomes as important as source production.

UNICEF has installed tanks in displacement areas across Gaza, reflecting the importance of distributed storage when piped distribution is unavailable.

The WASH repo should therefore treat barrels, cisterns, IBC totes, tanks, and jerry cans as key resilience assets.

A decentralized system can be expressed as:

```text
SOURCE A: trucked water ───────┐
SOURCE B: rainwater ───────────┤
SOURCE C: desalinated water ───┼──> CONTROLLED STORAGE
SOURCE D: treated well water ──┘
```

However, different sources should remain segregated until their quality is known.

Storage design must include:

- sealed lids;
- screened vents;
- controlled dispensing;
- cleaning access;
- source labeling;
- raw/treated separation;
- contamination inspection.

# 8. Wastewater failure and cross-contamination

Gaza demonstrates why water supply cannot be separated from sanitation.

World Bank damage assessments and humanitarian reporting have documented failure or damage of wastewater treatment plants, sewage pumping stations, and sewer networks.

WHO public-health assessment in late 2025 described overflowing sewage ponds, large quantities of uncollected waste, flood-prone displacement conditions, and elevated diarrhoeal disease burden.

UNICEF's 2026 reporting similarly identified sanitation failure, sludge-management disruption, and environmental contamination as major public-health risks.

## Design implication

Contested-zone water collection sites must be evaluated against sanitation hazards.

A rainwater collector placed beside:

- open sewage;
- waste piles;
- floodwater channels;
- latrines;
- animal waste;

may acquire contamination even if the collector itself is technically sound.

Site selection therefore needs a contamination-zone screen before buildout.

---

# 9. Flooding as both water opportunity and contamination event

Gaza receives strongly seasonal rainfall.

Historical literature commonly cites annual rainfall near 300-350 mm, with very little rainfall during the summer dry period.

Rain therefore cannot serve as the sole annual water source.

However, when storms occur, rainfall can generate substantial decentralized collection volumes.

At the same time, winter rainfall creates a major contamination hazard because damaged drainage, sewage accumulation, solid waste, and dense displacement settlements can produce polluted floodwater.

WHO reported that the 2025-2026 winter was expected to bring roughly 450-500 mm of rainfall into an environment with overflowing sewage ponds and large quantities of uncollected waste.

## Design implication

The repo should sharply distinguish:

```text
DIRECT RAINFALL COLLECTION
```

from:

```text
FLOODWATER COLLECTION
```

Direct rainfall intercepted before contact with contaminated ground is potentially valuable.

Floodwater in dense contested-zone settlements should generally be considered highly contaminated unless a rigorous treatment process exists.

# 10. Rainwater harvesting potential in Gaza

Historical Gaza literature suggests rainwater harvesting has not traditionally supplied a dominant share of household demand, largely because rainfall is seasonal and land/catchment space is limited.

That does not make it irrelevant under contested conditions.

The research question changes from annual self-sufficiency to **survival supplementation**.

Example:

A 4 x 4 ft collector has approximately 16 ft2 area.

One inch rainfall theoretically provides:

```text
16 x 0.623 ≈ 10 gallons
```

At 80% practical collection:

```text
≈ 8 gallons
≈ 30 litres
```

A four-pallet array:

```text
64 ft2
≈ 32 gallons / inch
≈ 121 litres / inch
```

Thus modest arrays can meaningfully supplement drinking/cooking or hygiene-water stocks during rainfall periods.

## Gaza-specific advantage

A freestanding pallet or tarp collector does not depend on:

- an intact roof;
- permanent housing;
- functioning gutters;
- municipal water pressure;
- grid power.

This makes the architecture especially relevant for displacement settings and damaged urban housing.

# 11. Solar energy and desalination

Solar-powered water treatment has a long research history in Gaza.

A 2001 study reported operation of a solar-powered desalination system at Al Azhar University in Gaza.

A 2008 review proposed solar-energy-driven water and power systems for Gaza.

A 2019 engineering study modeled a PV-powered seawater reverse-osmosis plant for southern Gaza and concluded that solar PV could economically support desalination at large scale under modeled conditions.

UNICEF's Gaza programming has also historically included solar-powered desalination prototypes and solar energy associated with larger desalination infrastructure.

## Design implication

Solar power is strongly aligned with regional WASH resilience because the solar resource remains available even when fuel or grid electricity is disrupted.

However, solar-powered RO still depends on:

- membranes;
- pumps;
- controls;
- pretreatment;
- spare parts;
- brine management.

Therefore solar energy reduces one dependency—fuel/electricity—but does not eliminate the treatment supply chain.

---

# 12. Public-health consequences of WASH collapse

WHO reporting in 2025 documented acute watery diarrhoea as a major reported condition in Gaza, associated with population displacement, poor WASH conditions, food-safety limitations, and malnutrition.

UNICEF reporting in 2026 also linked inadequate WASH conditions to hepatitis A, acute watery diarrhoea, skin infections, rodents, waste accumulation, and environmental contamination.

The system design implication is important:

```text
WASH IS NOT ONLY A WATER-VOLUME PROBLEM
```

A contested-zone WASH intervention should consider:

- drinking-water quantity;
- water quality;
- handwashing availability;
- sanitation;
- waste management;
- drainage;
- storage hygiene;
- disease surveillance.

---

# 13. Comparison: Syria

Syria provides a useful comparison because its WASH crisis combines prolonged conflict, damaged centralized systems, energy shortages, drought, and displacement.

UNICEF reported in 2026 that roughly 14.4 million people required WASH assistance.

In northeastern Syria, electricity damage affected water supply for large populations and increased dependence on generators and water trucking.

Syria also demonstrates the importance of **transitional WASH**: emergency water trucking combined with rehabilitation of wells, treatment plants, reservoirs, pumping stations, pipelines, and solarization.

## Transferable lesson

Do not freeze a contested-zone WASH system permanently at the emergency stage.

The progression should be:

```text
SURVIVAL
  ↓
STABILIZATION
  ↓
REHABILITATION
  ↓
RESILIENT RECOVERY
```

---

# 14. Comparison: Lebanon

Lebanon shows how conflict interacts with pre-existing economic and infrastructure fragility.

UNICEF reports that economic collapse and the 2024 and 2026 conflict escalations further weakened water and wastewater systems.

Solar-powered pumping and infrastructure rehabilitation have become major resilience strategies.

## Transferable lesson

WASH resilience systems should reduce operating-cost dependency, not only construction cost.

A system that is cheap to install but requires continual fuel, proprietary consumables, or inaccessible spares may be less resilient than a somewhat more expensive gravity/solar alternative.

---

# 15. Comparison: Yemen

Yemen demonstrates the interaction between conflict, chronic water scarcity, displacement, service disruption, and epidemic disease.

UNICEF reports more than 16 million people in need of WASH assistance, with cholera and other public-health threats worsened by service disruption and displacement.

## Transferable lesson

WASH systems in contested environments should be designed around **disease prevention**, not only water production.

This reinforces the need for:

- safe storage;
- chlorination or other validated disinfection where appropriate;
- sanitation controls;
- household hygiene support;
- microbial monitoring.

# 16. Contested-zone failure-mode taxonomy

The Gaza case supports the following general failure classes.

## F1 — source failure

Examples:

- well contamination;
- salinity;
- source inaccessible;
- source physically destroyed.

## F2 — energy failure

Examples:

- grid loss;
- generator failure;
- fuel shortage;
- lack of lubricants.

## F3 — treatment failure

Examples:

- desalination offline;
- chlorine unavailable;
- membrane failure;
- filter media exhausted.

## F4 — conveyance failure

Examples:

- pipeline break;
- pumping station loss;
- pressure loss.

## F5 — logistics failure

Examples:

- trucking route inaccessible;
- driver shortage;
- fuel shortage;
- spare parts blocked.

## F6 — storage failure

Examples:

- insufficient tanks;
- contaminated vessels;
- damaged reservoirs;
- open storage.

## F7 — sanitation failure

Examples:

- sewer damage;
- latrine shortage;
- sewage overflow;
- sludge accumulation.

## F8 — environmental failure

Examples:

- flooding;
- seawater intrusion;
- drought;
- waste accumulation.

## F9 — monitoring failure

Examples:

- laboratory unavailable;
- field test supplies exhausted;
- no chlorine/turbidity testing.

A resilient architecture should remain partially functional when one or more of these occur.

# 17. Research architecture derived from Gaza

The first WASH repo contested-zone architecture should therefore be:

```text
LOCAL COLLECTION
rain / protected source / trucked input
          ↓
SOURCE CLASSIFICATION
          ↓
RAW STORAGE
          ↓
SETTLING / ROUGHING
          ↓
CONTROLLED FILTRATION
          ↓
VALIDATED DISINFECTION
          ↓
SAFE STORAGE
          ↓
FIELD VERIFICATION
          ↓
BOUNDED DISTRIBUTION
```

The system should be modular enough to accept multiple sources while keeping unknown-quality water separated.

# 18. Gaza-specific prototype priorities

## Priority P1 — freestanding rain collector

Materials:

- pallet or scrap timber;
- waterproof liner or appropriate barrier coating;
- mesh;
- PVC or improvised gutter;
- first flush;
- existing barrel.

Goal:

Supplement scarce supplied water during rainfall without relying on intact buildings.

## Priority P2 — raw-water settling barrel

Goal:

Reduce sediment/turbidity before finer treatment.

## Priority P3 — gravity roughing filter

Goal:

Protect controlled downstream filters.

## Priority P4 — biosand / ceramic comparison

Goal:

Identify the most locally sustainable microbial-risk-reduction filter architecture.

## Priority P5 — controlled membrane drop-in

Goal:

Evaluate whether commercial hollow-fiber modules can add a stronger health-critical barrier while preserving locally improvised upstream components.

## Priority P6 — safe storage

Goal:

Prevent post-treatment recontamination in displacement environments.

## Priority P7 — solar low-power node

Goal:

Support pumping, UV, sensors, or other low-energy treatment without continual fuel dependency.

# 19. Key design conclusions

### Conclusion 1

Gaza demonstrates that centralized WASH can fail through cascading dependencies rather than through lack of raw water alone.

### Conclusion 2

Decentralized collection should supplement—not compete with—restoration of municipal wells, pipelines, desalination, and wastewater systems.

### Conclusion 3

Rainwater is seasonal in Gaza but can provide meaningful episodic household-scale volumes during acute shortage.

### Conclusion 4

Groundwater cannot be assumed usable because salinity and nitrate are persistent regional constraints.

### Conclusion 5

No-power and gravity treatment stages are valuable because fuel and electricity are recurrent failure points.

### Conclusion 6

Storage is a primary resilience technology.

### Conclusion 7

Wastewater and solid-waste failures can destroy gains from improved water supply through environmental contamination.

### Conclusion 8

Field testing is essential because contested-zone sources can shift rapidly in quality.
